The Agreement Gate

A cheaper way to decode a quantum memory: ask two quick decoders. If they agree, trust them. If they don't, call the expensive one.

The problem

Accurate decoders are slow on the hard cases

Errors leave clues

144 data qubits store 12 protected qubits. 144 helper qubits keep measuring parity checks. A check that changes between rounds is an alarm.

The decoder plays detective

It picks the most likely set of faults that explains the alarms. A wrong guess corrupts the stored information.

Accuracy

Matches Relay-BP

317 vs 327 failed pairs overall. At p = 0.005 slightly better (14 pairs only the gate got right, 2 only Relay-BP got right).

Average speed

Faster at p ≤ 0.004

0.48× at p = 0.003, 0.70× at p = 0.004. Controlled single-core timing at p = 0.004 gives 0.79×, or 0.72× for the lean version.

Limits

High noise and slow tail

At p = 0.005, 11% of shots escalate and the saving disappears. The slowest 1% get 13–23% slower at p ≥ 0.004.

What we learned on the way
  • When plain belief propagation (BP) converges, it was right 4,923 times out of 4,923.
  • Skipping easy shots barely helps: the time is spent on the hard 38%.
  • A model reading one decoder's confidence could not tell when a valid answer was the wrong one.
  • A second, independently seeded run does tell: disagreement flags ambiguous shots.
  • The lean version's times are built from measured per-decoder timings. It still needs its own end-to-end run.
How we tested it
  • Pass/fail rules written down before the results were read.
  • New simulation seeds; every decoder run on exactly the same shots.
  • Noise p = 0.003 (8,000 pairs), 0.004 (2 × 3,000), 0.005 (3,000).
  • Timing rechecked on one core with decoders in random order.
  • Reviewed by three AI models (Claude, GPT, Gemini) in five rounds.
